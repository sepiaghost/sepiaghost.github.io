Does differential privacy hurt rare cancer subtypes?

I trained cancer classifiers with differential privacy. The headline accuracy looked fine. It was hiding who pays for the privacy. Here's what I built, what broke, and what I'd do differently.

The easy task that told me nothing

My first project took differential privacy from one released statistic to a whole trained neural network. Differential privacy protects the people in a dataset by adding calibrated noise. DP-SGD applies that to training. It clips each sample's gradient to a maximum norm, adds Gaussian noise before every update, and tracks a privacy budget called epsilon (ε). Smaller ε means stronger privacy.

I trained a small network (2000 → 64 → 16 → 2) on TCGA-BRCA gene expression to separate tumor from normal tissue. The cohort had 1,231 samples (1,118 tumor, 113 normal), and I kept the 2,000 most variable genes. The non-private baseline reached 98.8% accuracy. Then I swept ε from 0.5 to 20.

The result looked almost too good. AUC stayed near 0.998 at every budget. At ε = 0.5, accuracy was still 98.8%. It didn't move in a clean line either. It bounced between 98.8% and 99.6%, which I put down to noise: each ε was a single run, and the test set had only 247 samples, where one flipped sample is about 0.4%.

It was tempting to write “DP-SGD costs almost nothing.” But a task this easy can't show a cost. Tumor and normal tissue differ so much in expression that a model can separate them through a lot of noise. The experiment was too easy to answer my question.

A harder question

So I made the task harder and asked something sharper: does DP-SGD cost every group equally?

The new task was classifying four PAM50 breast cancer subtypes in 821 TCGA-BRCA tumors. The subtypes are unevenly sized, and telling them apart is much subtler than tumor versus normal.

I also tightened the method: ten random splits instead of one, a non-private baseline, and budgets from ε = 0.5 to 8. I tracked overall accuracy and recall for each subtype, meaning the share of that subtype's samples the model actually found.

What broke

Overall accuracy told a mild story: 0.85 without privacy, 0.79 at ε = 8. Skim that and you'd say privacy cost about six points and move on.

The per-subtype numbers said otherwise. HER2, one of the smaller subtypes, collapsed:

No privacy0.84

At ε ≤ 2 the model found essentially no HER2 samples. The larger subtypes stayed high, so the average hid the damage. Privacy noise didn't hurt everyone evenly. It fell hardest on the smaller groups, and in a clinical setting, missing a rarer subtype is a real cost.

This confused me at first because HER2 AUROC stayed high. The model could still rank HER2 samples sensibly, so the signal was there. At the decision threshold, though, the noisy model defaulted to the larger classes and the rare one disappeared.

A fix, and where it stops working

Since the ranking survived, I tried a post-hoc class-prior correction. It adjusts predictions for how common each subtype is, so the rare class isn't drowned out. One catch: the class counts are themselves information about patients. I released them with a Laplace mechanism so that cost is counted too. At ε = 8 the total came to about 8.1.

It helped a lot. At ε = 8, HER2 recall went from 0.28 to 0.76, and the correction recovered most of the lost HER2 recall across ε = 2 to 8. The pattern held across three clipping norms, so it wasn't a quirk of one setting.

It has clear limits. At ε ≤ 1 the correction fails, and it does little for LumB, another subtype that suffered. It patches one specific failure. It isn't a fairness guarantee.

Methodology

Both projects use TCGA-BRCA gene expression and train a small feedforward network with DP-SGD in Opacus (PyTorch). Everything below comes from the two repo READMEs.

Project 1: tumor vs. normal

  • Data: TCGA-BRCA RNA-seq (STAR-Counts), pulled with TCGAbiolinks. 1,231 samples: 1,118 tumor, 113 normal.
  • Preprocessing: CPM, then log2. I kept the 2,000 most variable genes out of 60,660 and standardized them.
  • Split: stratified 80/20, which leaves 247 test samples.
  • Model: MLP, 2000 → 64 → 16 → 2.
  • Privacy: DP-SGD clips each sample's gradient to a maximum norm, then adds Gaussian noise. I swept ε over 0.5, 1, 3, 5, 10 and 20.
  • Metrics: accuracy and AUC. The non-private baseline reached 0.988 accuracy and 0.998 AUC.
  • Runs: one training run per ε.

Project 2: PAM50 subtypes

  • Data: TCGA-BRCA from the UCSC Xena hub, log2(RSEM + 1). I kept primary tumors with a LumA, LumB, Her2 or Basal call and dropped Normal-like because it is too small to evaluate. That leaves 821 tumors: LumA 421, LumB 192, Basal 141, Her2 67. Labels come from the PAM50Call_RNAseq column.
  • Features: the 500 highest-variance genes, chosen on the training split only, then standardized.
  • Splits: ten random stratified 75/25 splits of the same 821 tumors.
  • Model and training: MLP, 500 → 128 → 64 → 4, trained with Adam at a learning rate of 1e-3 for 30 epochs, batch size 64.
  • Privacy: target ε of 8, 4, 2, 1 and 0.5, with δ = 1 / training set size. The main results clip at a norm of 1.0, and a sweep covers 0.5, 1 and 2.
  • Class-prior correction: I divide the predicted probabilities by a class prior before taking the argmax. The prior comes from training label counts released with a Laplace mechanism (sensitivity 1, ε = 0.1). The total cost is the training ε plus 0.1, which is why ε = 8 becomes about 8.1.
  • Metrics: recall per subtype, one-vs-rest AUROC, overall accuracy, and the recall gap (best subtype recall minus worst). I report means and 95% intervals across the ten splits.

What I would do differently

My takeaway is simple: report results per group, not just on average. An average can look fine while one group quietly loses.

The work has real limits. These are what I'd fix next:

  • Single runs in the first project. Each ε was one training run on a 247-sample test set, so small differences are noise. I would average over many seeds and use a larger, more balanced test set. The second project uses ten random splits for this reason.
  • Few HER2 samples in the second project. Only 67 of the 821 tumors are HER2, and each test split holds about 17. One missed patient moves recall by about six points. The ten splits also resample the same 821 tumors, so they aren't independent. I'd want more HER2 cases before trusting the exact numbers.
  • Partial privacy accounting. In the second project, gene selection and standardization use the training data, but I didn't charge them to the privacy budget. The reported ε covers the training loop, plus 0.1 for the noisy class prior. I also didn't charge hyperparameter choice, though I fixed those in advance and never tuned them on test data. Read the ε values with that in mind.
  • Labels derived from expression. PAM50 subtypes are themselves computed from gene expression, so they are not an independent ground truth.
  • One cohort. Everything here is TCGA-BRCA. I would want to see whether the same pattern holds on other cancers and other datasets.

The code for both projects is public: differentially private deep learning for cancer classification and does DP-SGD hurt rare cancer subtypes?.
