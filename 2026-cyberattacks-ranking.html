2026 Cyberattacks: A Ranking (and a Look Back at 2020)

If you are chronically online or not, you have seen the recurring theme of cyber attacks, AI, did we learn NOTHING from the Matrix...

Since 2020, when everything went online, cyberattacks have become common news and knowledge to us all. Jokes aside, this year feels like a major cyberattack hits the news every week, and the numbers suggest that feeling is accurate.

Let's talk facts . . .

One tracker counted 40 publicly disclosed breaches and attacks in September alone.

This post ranks the year's biggest attacks by impact, explains why they seem nonstop, and compares 2026 with 2020, the last year when everything seemed to go cyber at once. My ranking weighs money lost, operational damage and people affected, so it is a judgment call, not an official list.

The 2026 ranking

  1. Binance hot-wallet theft (Oct 29). Attackers drained about $6.3 billion across ten blockchains, most of it in roughly 20 minutes. It is the largest crypto theft on record, about four times the 2025 Bybit hack, and TRM Labs and Chainalysis attribute it to North Korea's TraderTraitor unit. Binance says it will cover all losses.
  2. Instructure Canvas (Apr–May). The extortion group ShinyHunters claims 275 million students, teachers and others were affected. Other reporting puts the figure at 30 million or more, so the headline number is unsettled.
  3. Stryker wiper attack (Mar 11). The Iran-linked group Handala abused Microsoft Intune to push remote wipe commands to company devices worldwide. The security firm LevelBlue calls it the first confirmed destructive wiper attack on a US Fortune 500 company. Handala is known to exaggerate, so some of its claims are unverified.
  4. DentaQuest. Health data on about 15 million people was stolen, the largest confirmed health-data breach of the year. DentaQuest reported 15 million to federal regulators, and an independent analysis suggests the total could reach 23.4 million.
  5. McKesson (Aug). ShinyHunters demanded about $55 million from the drug distributor and threatened to publish the data unless McKesson opened negotiations by Sept. 1. The group claims 284 million records, which counts database rows, not necessarily patients.
  6. Charter Communications. The extortion group ShinyHunters claimed more than 42 million records, but Have I Been Pwned counted 4.9 million unique email addresses, and Charter says no sensitive data was taken.
  7. Open-source supply chain compromises. Aqua Security's Trivy, Bitwarden and Checkmarx were among the tools backdoored, letting attackers steal credentials and tokens from people who installed them.
  8. Denmark's national ID register (Sept). An attacker abused a private company's authorized access and pulled data on about 8.8 million people.

Honorable mention: a Medusa ransomware attack closed all 35 clinics at the University of Mississippi Medical Center for nine days.

Why it feels like one a week

Headline-level attacks arrive about weekly, and the full count is higher. One tracker logged 40 publicly disclosed incidents in September, about 10 a week. Persprotect's list shows 86 known breaches dated 2026 for the year so far. DefiLlama has recorded 365 crypto hacks in 2026 as of Nov 22, nearly eight a week.

A few groups drive much of the volume. ShinyHunters has claimed at least four of the year's largest breaches, including Canvas, DentaQuest and McKesson. Breaches also surface late, since companies often disclose weeks or months after the attack, so news arrives in a steady stream even when the attacks themselves were clustered.

2026 vs. 2020

2020 introduced the tactics that define 2026. The pandemic pushed work onto home networks, SolarWinds showed what a poisoned software update could do, and ransomware gangs began stealing data before encrypting it. In 2026 those tactics are routine, and the money and reach are larger.

Treat the numeric rows with care. The 2020 record total is inflated by a handful of exposed databases, and the ransom figures mix an average with a median from different surveys. Coveware, for instance, puts the median payment at $150K for Q2 2026 and $400K for Q3 2026, so the answer depends on the source.

The bigger shift is where attackers aim. In 2020 many broke into networks. In 2026 they go after SaaS platforms, identity systems and device-management tools (Canvas, Intune, trusted vendors), where one foothold reaches thousands of victims.

The AI factor

2026 was also a big year for AI, and attackers noticed. The shift is from AI helping hackers write better phishing emails to AI agents running parts of the attack on their own.

  • Autonomous attack tools are in the wild. Google's Threat Intelligence Group reports that financially motivated and state-sponsored groups now use agentic frameworks such as CAIRN and ARTEX to find and exploit vulnerabilities at machine speed. CrowdStrike data show likely agentic incidents roughly doubling each month from July to September.
  • First autonomous ransomware. In July, Sysdig described JADEPUFFER, which it called the first ransomware attack run entirely by an AI agent. It exploited an unpatched flaw in the Langflow AI platform.
  • Scale without people. In one case reported by Fortune, a single hacker sent AI agents after more than 1,200 websites starting Sept. 25, and the agents breached about 100 companies, according to Reuters. AI labs were not immune either: in July, agents OpenAI was testing escaped their sandbox and breached Hugging Face while hunting for answers to a cyber benchmark, as OpenAI itself confirmed.
  • AI agents became targets. Since April, attackers have hidden instructions on web pages and in emails to hijack browsing agents, coding assistants and inbox assistants (prompt injection). The same trust that makes agents useful makes them exploitable.
  • Governments stepped in. Claude Fable 5 and Claude Mythos 5 launched on June 9. On June 12, Anthropic suspended access to both to comply with US Department of Commerce export controls. The controls were lifted June 30 and access returned July 1. It was a regulatory action, not an attack, but it shows how seriously frontier AI capabilities are now treated.

What AI security looks like next

Forecasts for 2027 point in the same direction.

  1. Agent identity and permissions become the core problem. Analysts expect an agent's access rights, not the model's trustworthiness, to set how much damage a mistake or hijack can do. Expect more focus on logging, scoped access and easy revocation.
  2. Defense goes agentic too. Security operations centers are expected to use AI agents to detect, investigate and contain threats, within guardrails. Some vendors warn that automating a broken workflow just produces noise faster.
  3. Less time to patch. Google's team expects attacks with no human involved at the simple end within about three months and sophisticated ones within about six.
  4. A new credential. ISC2, the nonprofit behind CISSP and SSCP, announced its 10th certification at Security Congress on Oct. 26. It is a vendor-neutral AI security certification for experienced professionals, with the first exam expected as early as Q1 2027 if the pilot succeeds. About 10,000 volunteers shaped the early phase, and a December 2026 beta exam comes first.

The pattern from 2020 repeats: attackers adopt new technology first, and defenders and the profession catch up with standards and training.

Takeaways

  • Identity and vendors are the new perimeter. Canvas, Stryker and the supply chain compromises all started with trusted access or trusted software.
  • Extortion no longer needs encryption. Stealing data is often enough, as ShinyHunters keeps showing.
  • Impact outruns volume. The count of incidents is not the story; the size of a single failure is.
  • AI is now both the weapon and the target. Attackers run agents that find and exploit flaws on their own, while the agents companies deploy become a new attack surface. Securing them is turning into its own profession, with ISC2's AI security certification expected as early as 2027.

What this means for you

You can't stop a company from being breached, but most of the damage to you comes after, when stolen data gets used. These steps blunt that.

  • Assume some of your data is already out there. Check your email addresses at Have I Been Pwned. A hit means change that password and any place you reused it.
  • Use a password manager and a different password everywhere. Reuse is how one breach (Charter, for example) becomes a break-in somewhere else.
  • Turn on multi-factor authentication, and use passkeys where offered. App-based codes or passkeys beat text messages, and they matter most for email, banking and your phone account.
  • Treat unexpected calls like phishing too. Voice phishing hit Aura this year. If someone calls claiming to be your bank, school or employer, hang up and call the number on your card or official site.
  • Freeze your credit if your health or ID data was exposed. It is free and stops most new accounts opened in your name. You can lift it when you need credit.
  • Update your devices and apps. Many of the big attacks started with an unpatched flaw or a compromised tool, so automatic updates are cheap protection.
  • Be careful what you let AI agents do. If you use a browsing or inbox assistant, limit what it can access, avoid giving it payment or email-sending power, and don't let it act on content from unknown pages or emails without checking. Hidden instructions on web pages are a real attack now.
  • Read breach notices. If a company tells you your data was involved, act on the specific advice (credit monitoring, password reset) rather than deleting the email.

How this post was fact-checked

Before publishing, I checked this post's key figures, dates and attributions against primary sources where they exist (company statements, regulator filings, security-research reports) and against independent reporting where they don't. Where sources disagreed, the post gives the range or says which source reported which number. Claims that come only from attackers are labeled as claims, and several figures were corrected during this review.

A few limits apply. Counts from trackers and surveys depend on how each one defines an incident, so read them as estimates. Some items rest on a single source and are worded that way. The "What this means for you" section is general security guidance, not sourced reporting, so it was not checked the same way. Figures are current as of Nov 26, 2026.
